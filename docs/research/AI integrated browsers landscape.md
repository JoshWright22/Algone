# Incumbents Absorb the AI Browser Boom

As of October 2026, about ten AI-native browsers are active, led by Perplexity Comet, Dia, Opera Neon, Fellou, Genspark, Sigma, Strawberry, Norton Neo, BrowserOS and Polar. Every mainstream browser except Vivaldi now ships an assistant: Chrome, Edge, Safari, Firefox, Brave, Opera and Samsung Browser. The category's biggest name is gone, because **OpenAI shut down ChatGPT Atlas on August 9, 2026**, under ten months after launch, and moved its functions into the ChatGPT desktop app and a Chrome extension. Page chat, summaries and multi-tab context are now free commodities, while autonomous agents are the paid, gated layer, typically around $20 a month and, at Google and Microsoft, US-only. That layer is also where value and risk diverge: the sidebar is useful and low-risk, while logged-in autonomous action is the least reliable function and the most dangerous. Researchers have repeatedly hijacked agents in lab settings, and OpenAI, Anthropic and Brave all say the underlying problem is not fixed, though the research found no confirmed consumer loss from an AI browser. For most people the best choice is the browser they already use, plus its assistant or an extension. A dedicated AI browser makes sense only for a specific workflow, in a separate profile, away from email, password managers and saved cards. The evidence has limits: there is no independent cross-vendor benchmark, no reliable usage data for most products, and most security research comes from firms that sell security or compete in browsers.

## Atlas died, and about ten AI-native browsers remain

Atlas launched October 21, 2025, on macOS and never reached Windows or mobile; OpenAI announced its retirement in July 2026 and folded it into a single desktop app ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ChatGPT_Atlas); [9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/07/09/openai-is-discontinuing-chatgpt-atlas-its-standalone-desktop-browser/)). Any roundup that still recommends Atlas is stale. The closure is the strongest adoption signal in the category, though consolidation inside OpenAI explains part of it, so it is not clean proof that users rejected the product. Late-2025 data shows why the signal is surprising: Cyberhaven's customer data put Atlas at 62 times Comet's corporate downloads ([Cyberhaven](https://www.cyberhaven.com/press-releases/cyberhaven-labs-reports-24-of-enterprises-have-installed-chatgpt-atlas-new-agentic-browser-poses-emerging-security-risks)). Early curiosity did not secure the product's future.

The survivors have separated by audience. Comet is the consumer agent, Dia, Strawberry and Polar target knowledge work, Neon and Fellou target power users, Sigma and BrowserOS sell local control, and Norton Neo sells security. Three facts complicate the table. Dia's Windows installer called the release an "early, untested build that is not yet security-tested," which neither its Windows page nor its changelog explains ([WindowsForum](https://windowsforum.com/news/dia-ai-browser-arrives-on-windows-but-installer-warning-raises-security-questions.447495/)). Comet is the only startup-built agent browser with generally available iOS and Android apps confirmed on a primary page. And every browser with a verified engine runs Chromium, so extension import is the usual switching pitch, but confirmation is uneven: Neon's pages do not mention extensions at all, and Genspark and Polar remain unverified. Adjacent products sit outside this table, including Island, the enterprise browser valued at $6.4B ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/24/island-ai-cybersecurity-funding.html)), and Alibaba's Quark, whose browser agent features could not be verified.

Two 2026 shifts stand out. Microsoft retired Copilot Mode as a brand in May and built its features into Edge, and Mozilla added a browser-wide kill switch after a user backlash ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/03/firefox_ai_kill_switch/)). A clear off switch is now a trust feature, and Chrome lacks one in its documentation. Geography is the other gap: Chrome's assistant reportedly reached 187 locales by August with no EU state on the list, a secondary count not checked against Google's full page ([Keywords Everywhere](https://keywordseverywhere.com/news/gemini-updates/gemini-in-chrome/)). Bundling goes further than any browser's own sidebar, too. Gemini Spark gained Chrome auto browse on July 30, US first ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2026/07/30/gemini-spark-chrome-auto-browse/)).

Developers get a thinner slice than users. Chrome offers stable on-device Summarizer, Translator, Language Detector and Prompt APIs, with the Prompt API available to extensions since Chrome 138, but these run on Gemini Nano, are desktop-only, and need 22 GB of free disk plus a GPU above 4 GB of VRAM or 16 GB of RAM ([Chrome docs](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/ai/get-started); [Chrome APIs](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/ai/built-in-apis)). No documented API giving extensions access to cloud Gemini in Chrome or auto browse surfaced, though that search was not exhaustive.

## Pricing moved from browser fees to metered agent work

The browser itself is now close to free across the category. Comet dropped its paywall in late 2025 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet_(browser))), and Neon added a free plan in August 2026, keeping its own agents on Standard ([Opera](https://blogs.opera.com/news/2026/08/your-ai-agents-can-use-opera-neon-free-of-charge/)). The money has moved to two price bands: an entry tier from $10 to $25, and a heavy-user tier from $100 to $250. Brave's Leo Premium, reported at $14.99 ([eesel](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/brave-leo-pricing)), fits the lower band. Prices for Google AI Pro and Ultra and Microsoft 365 Premium were not collected, so the incumbent agent gate cannot be compared directly.

Metering makes those list prices hard to compare. **Dia replaced its $20 flat Pro plan with $10 and $100 tiers plus $20 credit blocks**, which I read as evidence that flat AI pricing did not hold, although Dia has not said so. One review also found $20 in a Dia FAQ against the $10 plan display, so check at checkout ([Dia](https://www.diabrowser.com/pricing); [WindowsForum](https://windowsforum.com/news/dia-ai-browser-arrives-on-windows-but-installer-warning-raises-security-questions.447495/)). Fellou's 1,000-Spark free allowance is estimated at about four tasks, which would make its 2,000-Spark $19 plan roughly eight tasks a month, though Fellou warns usage "varies significantly." Strawberry's credits cost about a third of a cent on every plan against one cent as top-ups, and monthly credits do not roll over ([Strawberry](https://strawberrybrowser.com/pricing)). Few vendors publish task counts, so buyers cannot price an agent task before they run one.

A newer model avoids selling AI at all. Neon's free MCP and CLI access, BrowserOS neo and Strawberry's ChatGPT connection all treat the browser as a surface for an agent you already pay for. For anyone with an existing AI subscription, this removes the second bill.

## Most major agents have been hijacked in the lab, but no victim is confirmed

The attack class is simple: an agent cannot reliably tell the user's instructions from text on a page, and it holds the user's logged-in sessions. Conventional bugs in AI components get clean fixes. Injection flaws get mitigations that are often bypassed later. OpenAI says prompt injection is "unlikely to ever be fully 'solved,'" and the UK NCSC says it "may never be totally mitigated" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/12/22/openai-says-ai-browsers-may-always-be-vulnerable-to-prompt-injection-attacks/)).

| Date | Target | Researcher | Demonstrated | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aug 2025 | Comet | [Brave](https://brave.com/blog/comet-prompt-injection/) | Hidden Reddit comment led to account takeover after one "Summarize" click | Brave says not fully mitigated on retest |
| Aug 2025 | Comet | [Guardio](https://guard.io/labs/scamlexity-we-put-agentic-ai-browsers-to-the-test-they-clicked-they-paid-they-failed) | Bought a watch on a fake store with saved card details | No retest found |
| Oct 2025 | Comet, Fellou, Neon | [Brave](https://brave.com/blog/unseeable-prompt-injections/) | Commands in near-invisible screenshot text and page content | Perplexity disputed Brave's framing; fix status unstated |
| Mar 2026 | Chrome Gemini panel | [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/03/03/google_chrome_bug_gemini) | CVE-2026-0628: malicious extension reached camera, mic and local files | Fixed Jan 2026 |
| Mar 2026 | Comet | [Zenity](https://zenity.io/blog/security/perplexedbrowser-accepting-a-meeting-or-handing-your-local-files-to-an-attacker) | Calendar invite led to local file theft and a 1Password takeover path | Zenity says fixed before disclosure |
| Mar 2026 | Claude for Chrome | [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/03/claude-extension-flaw-enabled-zero.html) | Zero-click injection from any site | Fixed in 1.0.41 |
| Jul 2026 | Atlas, Claude, Comet, Fellou, Genspark, Sigma | [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/bioshocking-ai-browser-credential-leak-layerx) | False "game rules" made agents copy credentials | LayerX: OpenAI fixed, Anthropic patch failed, Perplexity closed the report, three no reply; a Genspark fix claim conflicts |

Comet has the longest record, which reflects its early launch and agentic depth as well as its handling. Its disputed or closed reports come mainly from the companies that filed them, and those companies sell security products or, in Brave's case, compete. No comparable public disclosure surfaced for Edge, which does not show that none exists.

Defenses are real but unaudited. Google's design has a separate critic that checks proposed actions without seeing raw web content, plus limits on which sites the agent can touch ([Google](https://blog.google/security/architecting-security-for-agentic/?hl=en)). Brave isolates AI browsing in its own profile and still says safeguards reduce rather than eliminate injection. Anthropic reports attack success of 1.4% on Opus 4.5 with safeguards ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/prompt-injection-defenses)), and later reports cite 0.5% and 0% ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/security/anthropic-browser-agent-hijacked-31-percent-before-safeguards-engaged); [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/opus-5-may-have-solved-browser-based-prompt-injection-the-biggest-security-flaw-haunting-ai-agents/)). These are vendor numbers from known test sets, and they do not bound the risk from novel attacks across millions of page loads. Disclosure: Anthropic is a vendor in this market and the maker of the model that wrote this report, so its figures deserve the same discount as anyone else's.

Attackers are paying attention. Unit 42 saw live pages using 22 delivery techniques, including forced purchases ([Unit 42](https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/ai-agent-prompt-injection/)), and Google measured a 32% relative rise in malicious injection from November 2025 to February 2026, without sophisticated coordinated campaigns ([Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/04/24/indirect-prompt-injection-in-the-wild/)). Even so, a fintech review found no sourced production loss from agent payments ([Finextra](https://www.finextra.com/blogposting/33090/when-agents-pay-the-wrong-party-what-the-sourced-public-record-shows-so-far-in-2026)). That absence is weak comfort, since hijacked agent actions look like the user's own. The risk also follows capability: the Cloud Security Alliance calls a summarize-only assistant low-risk, and it ranks agents with email, terminal or payment access as the higher-priority targets ([CSA](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-indirect-prompt-injection-in-the-wild-2026/)).

Routine privacy exposure is certain rather than hypothetical. Google states that Gemini in Chrome collects page content and URLs from shared tabs, keeps activity 18 months by default, and retains human-reviewed chats up to three years even after deletion ([Google](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961)). Perplexity's policy is broader and silent on page content: it covers improving "AI models," says Incognito does not prevent tracking, and allows sharing of identifiers and internet activity with advertising partners ([Perplexity](https://www.perplexity.ai/hub/legal/privacy-policy)). A class action alleges Perplexity's trackers sent conversations to Meta and Google; that claim is unproven and concerns the service generally, not Comet ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/perplexity-ai-sued-over-alleged-data-sharing-with-meta-and-google/)). Policies for Dia, Neon, Fellou, Genspark and Sigma were not reviewed, and neither were the terms for ChatGPT's replacement desktop browser.

Enterprises still reference Gartner's December 2025 advice to "block AI browsers for now" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/12/08/gartner_recommends_ai_browser_ban/)). The product context has aged, since Atlas is gone and defenses have improved, but its structural reason has not: an employee's agent inherits that employee's SaaS sessions. No survey shows how many enterprises complied. Liability is also unsettled. Google reportedly tells users they remain responsible for wrong clicks and unintended purchases ([The Tech Buzz](https://www.techbuzz.ai/articles/google-s-auto-browse-ai-agent-falls-short-in-real-world-tests)), and the Ninth Circuit lifted Amazon's injunction against Comet's shopping agent on the reasoning that the user, not Perplexity, accessed the site. The merits remain open ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/417007/appeals-court-sides-against-amazon-lifts-perplexi.html?edition=)).

## The value sits in the sidebar, not the agent

The safest function is also the most useful one. Summaries, research synthesis and multi-tab comparison work well enough to justify daily use, and they account for much of real demand. In a study co-authored by Perplexity using Comet data, productivity plus learning and research made up 57% of agentic queries ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.07828)), and publishers drew 43.5% of agent traffic in July 2026 per HUMAN ([HUMAN](https://www.humansecurity.com/learn/blog/state-of-agentic-traffic-july-2026-publishers-claim-highest-share-of-agentic-traffic/)). Agents are being used mostly to read.

Autonomous action remains slow and uneven. Atlas reportedly took about 10 minutes to add three items to an Amazon cart, against 45 seconds by hand and two minutes for Comet, in a secondhand account of The Verge's test ([hlrnet reprint](https://hlrnet.com/sites/ai/?p=14447)). Wired's test of Chrome auto browse partly succeeded on symphony tickets and gave up on campground research after 15 minutes ([The Tech Buzz](https://www.techbuzz.ai/articles/google-s-auto-browse-ai-agent-falls-short-in-real-world-tests)). A solo tester after Google I/O 2026 got measurable time savings in four of five runs, a small sample that suggests the product is improving ([Sean Kim](https://blog.imseankim.com/chrome-auto-browse-gemini-agentic-hotel-form-fill-google-io-2026/)). The best agent in a 2025 academic benchmark finished 61.3% of 300 live-site tasks, and most managed about 30% ([paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01382v4); [TestMu](https://www.testmuai.com/blog/state-of-ai-browser-agents-2026/)). Those papers predate current products, but the arithmetic still applies: 90% success per step yields under 35% over ten steps.

The steelman for agents is that minutes spent in the background are not minutes spent by the user, so "slower than a human" overstates the cost. That holds only if you can walk away, and safe use requires the opposite: confirmations and supervision on anything sensitive. This trade-off is the central flaw in the category's pitch. Tedious, reversible jobs on well-structured sites fit today's agents. Unstated preferences, availability across many sites, bulk data entry and messy dynamic pages do not, and neither does anything involving money or credentials without review. No controlled time trial of current products exists, so these judgments rest on anecdote and older benchmarks.

Adoption data supports the same conclusion. Standalone AI browsers do not appear in StatCounter, though Chromium browsers presenting a Chrome user agent may be counted as Chrome. Comet user estimates for Q1 2026 differ sixfold, from about 3 million ([index.dev](https://www.index.dev/blog/perplexity-ai-features-statistics)) to 18 million ([Presenc](https://presenc.ai/research/comet-usage-statistics-2026)), and neither was verified at source. Comet still led agent traffic among HUMAN's customers at 48.12% in April and 40.7% in August ([HUMAN](https://humansecurity.com/learn/blog/state-of-agentic-traffic-april-26); [HUMAN](https://www.humansecurity.com/learn/blog/state-of-agentic-traffic-august-2026-agentic-traffic-grows-27-reaches-new-high-as-codex-debuts-strongly/)), which is a share of a small category. Claude's Chrome extension meanwhile reportedly climbed from about 40,000 installs to over 10 million in six months ([SearchVIU](https://www.searchviu.com/en/ai-browsers-2026-compared/)). Installs are not active use, but the direction fits the thesis that people want agents without changing browsers.

These recommendations are my judgment from the evidence above, not test results.

| If you are | Choose | Why, and the catch |
|---|---|---|
| Most users | Your current browser's assistant, or an extension | No switching cost; agent access depends on region and plan |
| Consumer wanting the broadest agent | Comet, in a separate profile with no saved cards | Widest platform reach; longest vulnerability record and broad data terms |
| Knowledge worker on Slack, Notion, GitHub | Dia | Strongest work-tool context on Mac; treat the Windows build as early |
| Small sales, support or recruiting team | Strawberry, as a trial | Approval gates and shared companions; 12 staff, and 1.0 is days old |
| Already paying for an AI agent | Neon free tier or BrowserOS neo | No second subscription; you own the security model |
| Privacy-first user who wants AI | Brave, or local-first BrowserOS | Brave publishes its isolation design and no-retention claims; Sigma did not reply to LayerX |
| AI-averse user | Firefox with AI blocked, or Vivaldi | Clearest refusal options available |
| Enterprise | Block agents by default, then pilot under a risk assessment | Gartner's reasoning still holds; Dia reports SOC 2 Type II and supports Chromium enterprise policies |

## Conclusion

The contest has shifted from browser against browser to AI layer against AI layer, and an AI layer no longer needs to own a browser to win. OpenAI's route now resembles Anthropic's, with an extension running inside Chrome and, for OpenAI, Edge, Brave, Opera and Vivaldi as well, so Chrome's share overstates Google's control of what happens in its own windows. Startups face a harder squeeze: the free baseline keeps rising, frontier models are rentable by anyone, and Chromium removes engine differentiation. That leaves work-specific context, trust design and the role of neutral surface for other people's agents as the defensible ground, and the bring-your-own-agent model is the most underrated development of 2026.

The next question is who pays when an agent gets it wrong, not which model is smartest. Reliability and safety are in direct tension today, because the confirmations that keep agents safe also eliminate the time savings. Watch for three signals that would change this assessment: an independent cross-vendor injection benchmark, a first confirmed consumer loss, and card networks setting rules for agent-initiated payments. Google or Microsoft taking agents out of the US paywall at scale would also matter more to startups than any feature launch.
